\noindent\textbf{Course information}: This course is an upper-division undergraduate course and introductory graduate course on computational physics, focusing on \emph{stochastic} methods: Monte Carlo integration, Markov chain Monte Carlo, and the statistical analysis of simulation output.
We develop these methods in the setting of Feynman's path integral formulation of quantum mechanics, which turns quantum mechanical problems into high-dimensional integrals that are natural targets for sampling.
The course will explore both the theoretical foundations and computer implementations.
Students will develop their own code to solve the physics applications.
Basic knowledge of calculus, quantum mechanics, Linux, and programming in some language is expected.

The course structure will consist of weekly lectures on conceptual topics, e.g., quantum mechanics and Monte Carlo theory, and lab sections on computational tools, e.g., programming in Python and C/C++.
Students will learn how to apply physical reasoning to programming, optimize and debug code, create simulations of physical systems, and---critically for stochastic methods---report results with honest uncertainties.
Students will also learn how to use modern tools to efficiently solve scientific computing problems: interpreted (Python) vs.\ compiled (C/C++) languages and how to link the two.
There will be 3 individual homework assignments.
There will also be a final project in which students will work in groups.

\noindent\textbf{Relationship to PHYS 141/241}: \textbf{PHYS 141/241 and PHYS 142/242 are independent companion courses. Neither is a prerequisite for the other, and they may be taken in either order.}
In academic year 2026--27, PHYS 142/242 is offered in Fall and PHYS 141/241 is offered in Winter, so many of you will take this course first.
Nothing in this course assumes you have taken PHYS 141/241.

The two courses divide computational physics by \emph{methods}:
\begin{itemize}
  \itemsep-0.3em
  \item \textbf{PHYS 141/241 (deterministic methods)}: ODE integrators (Euler, Runge--Kutta, symplectic/leapfrog), stability and accuracy analysis, boundary value problems and shooting, PDE solvers (finite difference, Crank--Nicolson, spectral, finite element), deterministic $N$-body simulation.
  \item \textbf{PHYS 142/242 (stochastic methods, this course)}: pseudorandom number generation and sampling, Monte Carlo integration and variance reduction (importance sampling, VEGAS), Markov chains, the Metropolis--Hastings algorithm, detailed balance, autocorrelation and statistical error estimation, path integrals, and the imaginary-time mapping between quantum mechanics and statistical mechanics.
\end{itemize}
Week 9 of this course includes a lecture surveying the deterministic methods covered in PHYS 141/241 and how they relate to what we do here.

\noindent\textbf{Student learning outcomes}: Upon successful completion of Physics 142/242, students will be able to:
\begin{itemize}
  \itemsep-0.3em
  \item Design computer programs to numerically solve physics problems, like the harmonic oscillator using the Feynman path integral approach.
  \item Estimate and report statistical uncertainties on Monte Carlo results.
  \item Validate a stochastic calculation against an independent deterministic one and explain any residual discrepancy quantitatively.
  \item Consider multiple approaches and compare their computational performance, accuracy, and fidelity to physical laws.
  \item Find and choose the best tool or programming language for the task.
  \item Use AI coding assistants effectively and critically, and recognize its characteristic failure modes.
  \item Visualize the solutions.
  \item Collaborate with peers to tackle complex, realistic problems.
  \item Present findings, and defend the technical choices behind them.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent\emph{AI use appendix (required)}: Every homework and project submission must end with a short appendix, no more than half a page, containing:
\begin{enumerate}
  \itemsep-0.3em
  \item Which tools you used, and for what.
  \item The prompts you used, or a share link to the conversation.
  \item What the tool got wrong, and how you caught it.
\end{enumerate}
``I did not use AI tools on this assignment'' is a complete and perfectly acceptable appendix.
The appendix is worth points on each assignment.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent The third item is important.
Language models may be confidently wrong about numerical physics in different ways.
Finding those errors is an integral skill that we will spend a lab on (Lab 10).

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \emph{Enforcement}: Submitting AI-generated work with a false or missing AI use appendix, submitting another person's work as your own, or using unauthorized aids during a quiz or oral checkpoint are all violations and will be reported to the UCSD Office of Academic Integrity, and can result in an automatic ``F'' for the course at the discretion of the instructor.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent\emph{Collaboration with your peers} is encouraged---discuss concepts, approaches, and debugging freely.
But by the time you start writing your own solution to turn in, you should not be looking at anyone else's.
Plagiarizing a solution but changing variable names is cheating.
Soliciting solutions online (Chegg, Quora, course-answer sites) is cheating.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent Any questions on what constitutes an academic integrity violation should be addressed to the instructor.

\noindent\textbf{Homework}: There will be 3 homework assignments, submitted as code and a report on Gradescope.

\begin{itemize}
  \itemsep-0.3em
  \item \textbf{Parameters:} Some problems assign numerical parameters (e.g., potential shape, temperature, lattice size, or random seed) derived from your student ID.
  \item \textbf{Random seeds:} Every Monte Carlo result should be reported with the random seed that produced it and an uncertainty. We may download your code and re-run it to reproduce your results.
  \item \textbf{Failure modes:} Some problems may ask you to run a method in a regime where it works badly---a time step that is too large, a chain that is too short, etc.---and to diagnose what went wrong from the output.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Final project}:
For the final project, students will work in groups of $\sim$4.
The project deliverables are: (1) code provided as a public GitHub repository, (2) a 20-minute presentation by all members of the group, (3) a $\sim$4-page report in the Phys.\ Rev.\ Lett.\ two-column format (page limit does not apply to references and appendices), and (4) self and peer evaluations for group contributions.

Two notes:
\begin{itemize}
  \itemsep-0.3em
  \item \textbf{Q\&A:} Following each group presentation, there will be about 5 minutes of questions in which each group member should be able to answer questions about any part of the project. Divide the work, but make sure everyone understands the whole project.
  \item \textbf{Commit history:} Your repository should show the actual development of the project.
\end{itemize}
