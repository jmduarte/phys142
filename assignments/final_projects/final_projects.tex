\section{Double Well Potential with MCMC}

Consider the double well potential from Assignment 2,
\begin{equation}
  V(x) = \alpha x^4 - 2x^2 + \frac{1}{\alpha} = \alpha\left(x^2 - \frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^2,
\end{equation}
with $m=\hbar=1$ and $\alpha = 0.4$.
In Assignment 2 you watched a particle tunnel between the wells in real time and found the energy gap $\Delta E = E_1 - E_0$ from the eigenstates of the discretized Hamiltonian; Problem B(i) there already gives $E_0$ and $E_1$ at $\alpha = 0.4$~\cite{Banerjee:1978}.
In this project you will recover $E_0$, $\Delta E$, and the position distribution from imaginary-time paths alone, with your Assignment 2 eigenstates as the benchmark throughout.
See Refs.~\cite{Mittal:2020,Rodgers:2014} for discussions of similar problems, and Ref.~\cite{Creutz:1981} for an early MCMC study of this system.

As in Assignment 3, divide the imaginary time $\beta$ into $N$ slices of width $\delta\tau = \beta/N$.
A closed path $x = (x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_{N-1})$, with $x_N \equiv x_0$, has probability
\begin{equation}
  p(x) \propto e^{-S_E(x)}, \qquad
  S_E(x) = \sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\left[\frac{(x_{i+1}-x_i)^2}{2\,\delta\tau} + \delta\tau\, V(x_i)\right],
\end{equation}
which is the discretized path integral for $Z = \mathrm{Tr}\, e^{-\beta\hat H}$ (Lecture 13).
By the virial theorem, the energy can be estimated from the positions alone,
\begin{equation}
  \langle\hat H\rangle_\beta = \left\langle V(x) + \frac{1}{2}\,x\,V'(x)\right\rangle_\beta,
\end{equation}
averaged over slices and sampled paths.

\begin{problem}
  \emph{Ground state.}
  At large $\beta$, compute $E_0$ with the virial estimator and histogram the $x_i$ to obtain $|\phi_0(x)|^2$.
  Compare both with your Assignment 2 results, using error bars that account for autocorrelation (Lab 12).
  How large must $\beta$ be? (Hint: compare $\beta$ with $1/\Delta E$, not only with $1/(E_2 - E_0)$.)
  How do your results change with $\delta\tau$?
  Describe your strategy for $\beta$, $\delta\tau$, the initial configuration, burn-in, hit size (and resulting acceptance rate), number of sweeps, and thinning (if any).
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}
  \emph{Tunneling in imaginary time.}
  Measure the correlation function $C(\tau) = \langle x(0)\,x(\tau)\rangle_\beta$, averaged over all pairs of slices separated by $\tau$.
  Insert eigenstates to show that, once $\tau$ and $\beta-\tau$ are long enough that states above $\phi_1$ no longer contribute,
  \begin{equation}
    C(\tau) \approx |\langle\phi_0|x|\phi_1\rangle|^2\,\frac{\cosh\left[\Delta E\,(\tau-\beta/2)\right]}{\cosh(\beta\,\Delta E/2)}.
  \end{equation}
  Fit your $C(\tau)$ to extract $\Delta E$ and $|\langle\phi_0|x|\phi_1\rangle|$ with uncertainties, and compare with Assignment 2.
  Plot the time series of the path center $\bar x = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i x_i$ and a few sampled paths.
  How often does your chain move between the wells, and what does that imply for $\tau_\mathrm{int}$ and the number of sweeps you need?
  Does a move that shifts the whole path, $x_i \to x_i + d$ for all $i$ (the bonus problem of Assignment 3), help? Why or why not?
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}
  \emph{Finite temperature.}
  Repeat Problem A for several smaller values of $\beta$, from about $1/\Delta E$ down to $\beta < 1$.
  Compare your histograms with the thermal distribution built from the eigenstates,
  \begin{equation}
    \rho_\beta(x) = \frac{\sum_n e^{-\beta E_n}\,|\phi_n(x)|^2}{\sum_n e^{-\beta E_n}},
  \end{equation}
  and plot your energies against $\beta$ together with $\langle\hat H\rangle_\beta = \sum_n E_n e^{-\beta E_n}/\sum_n e^{-\beta E_n}$.
  How many eigenstates, and how large a box, do you need at your smallest $\beta$?
  What does $\rho_\beta(x)$ correspond to in statistical mechanics, and what should it approach as $\beta\to 0$?
\end{problem}

\noindent\textbf{Possible extensions:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A move that reflects a segment of the path, $x_i \to -x_i$ for $i_1 \leq i < i_2$.
        Which terms of $S_E$ change, and why is the proposal symmetric?
        Compare how often the chain tunnels, and the resulting $\tau_\mathrm{int}$, with and without it.
  \item The midpoint potential $\delta\tau\,V\!\left(\frac{x_i+x_{i+1}}{2}\right)$ used in the Assignment 2 propagator: does it converge faster or slower in $\delta\tau$?
  \item Repeat Problem B for several $\alpha$ and compare how $\Delta E$ falls with the barrier height $V(0) = 1/\alpha$ against the instanton (WKB) estimate.
\end{itemize}

\section{2D Ising Model with MCMC}

Consider the 2D Ising model of Assignment 3 on an $L\times L$ square lattice $\Lambda$ with periodic boundary conditions, now in the presence of an external magnetic field $B$.
The energy of the system for a given spin configuration $\sigma = \{\sigma_i\}_{i\in \Lambda}$ is
\begin{equation}
  E(\sigma) = -J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle} \sigma_i\sigma_j - B\sum_{i\in \Lambda} \sigma_i,
\end{equation}
where $\langle i j \rangle$ denotes two adjacent sites (with no double counting), $J$ is the spin-spin interaction, and $\sigma_i \in \{ -1, +1\}$ is the spin at site $i$.
As in Assignment 3, set $J = k_\mathrm{B} = 1$; the total magnetization and the magnetization per spin are
\begin{equation}
  M(\sigma) = \sum_{i\in \Lambda} \sigma_i, \qquad m(\sigma) = \frac{M(\sigma)}{|\Lambda|}.
\end{equation}
In Assignment 3, a $20\times 20$ lattice at $B=0$ did not show a sharp transition at $T_\mathrm{c}=\frac{2}{\ln(1+\sqrt{2})}\approx 2.269$.
Here you will map out the phase diagram on larger lattices and see how the transition sharpens as $L$ grows.
All results should have error bars that account for autocorrelation (Lab 12).

\begin{problem}
  Use Markov chain Monte Carlo and the Metropolis algorithm to simulate the model on a $100\times 100$ lattice at different temperatures $T$ and magnetic field strengths $B$.
  Discuss your strategy for determining the initial configuration, burn-in steps, total number of steps, and thinning (if any).
  At $B=0$, plot the integrated autocorrelation time of $m$ versus $T$ and discuss what it implies for runs near $T_\mathrm{c}$.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}
  Plot 1D scans of $m$ versus $T$ for fixed $B$ at three different values: $B<0$, $B=0$, and $B>0$.
  Plot 1D scans of $m$ versus $B$ for fixed $T$ at three different values: $T<T_\mathrm{c}$, $T=T_\mathrm{c}$, and $T>T_\mathrm{c}$.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}
  \emph{Finite-size effects.}
  At $B=0$, scan $T$ across the transition for at least three lattice sizes up to $L=100$, including $L=20$ from Assignment 3.
  Plot $\langle|m|\rangle$, the susceptibility per spin $\chi = \frac{|\Lambda|}{T}\left(\langle m^2\rangle - \langle|m|\rangle^2\right)$, and the heat capacity per spin $C = \frac{1}{|\Lambda|\,T^2}\left(\langle E^2\rangle - \langle E\rangle^2\right)$ versus $T$, with jackknife or bootstrap error bars for $\chi$ and $C$.
  (Lab 12 used $m$ in place of $|m|$; explain why $|m|$ is needed at $B=0$.)
  How do the positions and heights of the peaks change with $L$?
  Use these results to explain what you saw in Assignment 3.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}
  Putting this all together, draw/describe the phase diagram in $B$ versus $T$ of the 2D Ising model, where the magnetization $m$ is the order parameter.
  Consider discussing first-order phase transitions and critical exponents, and hysteresis and metastable states.
  See Refs.~\cite{Eastman:2014,Krauss:2018} for relevant discussions.
\end{problem}

\noindent\textbf{Possible extensions:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Cluster updates (e.g., the Wolff algorithm~\cite{Wolff:1989}) near $T_\mathrm{c}$: compare autocorrelation times with single-spin flips as $L$ grows.
  \item Locate $T_\mathrm{c}$ from the crossing of the Binder cumulant $U_L = 1 - \langle m^4\rangle / (3\langle m^2\rangle^2)$ for different $L$.
  \item Estimate the critical exponent $\gamma/\nu$ (exactly $7/4$) from how the peak of $\chi$ grows with $L$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Drell--Yan Event Generator with VEGAS}

Consider the Drell--Yan production process at an electron-positron collider, in which an electron and positron collide to produce a virtual photon or a $Z$ boson that then decays into a muon-antimuon pair, $e^+e^-\to Z/\gamma \to \mu^+\mu^-$.

\begin{center}
  \includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{eemumu.pdf}
  \includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{eemumukin.pdf}
\end{center}

As described in lecture and in Ref.~\cite{Papaefstathiou:2014mya}, the differential cross section for center-of-mass energy $E_\text{CM} = \sqrt{\hat s}$ and scattering angle $\theta$ is given by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:partonicxs}
  \frac{ d\sigma } { d \Omega }(\hat s, \cos\theta) = \frac{\alpha^2}{4 \hat{s} } \left[ A_0 (\hat s) ( 1  + \cos ^2 \theta ) + A_1(\hat s) \cos \theta \right] \;,
\end{equation}
where $A_0$ and $A_1$ are given by
\begin{align}
  A_0(\hat s) & = Q_e^2 - 2 Q_e V_\mu  V_e ~\chi_1(\hat s) + (A_\mu^2 + V_\mu^2)  (A_e^2 + V_e^2)  ~\chi_2(\hat s)\;, \nonumber \\
  A_1(\hat s) & = -4 Q_e  A_\mu A_e ~\chi_1(\hat s) + 8 A_\mu V_\mu A_e V_e ~ \chi_2(\hat s)\;,
\end{align}
and the $\chi_1$ and $\chi_2$ are given by
\begin{align}
  \chi_1 (\hat{s}) & = \kappa \hat{s} ( \hat{s} - M_Z^2 ) / (  (\hat{s}-M_Z^2)^2 + \Gamma_Z^2 M_Z^2 ) \;, \nonumber \\
  \chi_2 (\hat{s}) & = \kappa^2 \hat{s}^2 / (  (\hat{s}-M_Z^2)^2 + \Gamma_Z^2 M_Z^2 ) \;, \nonumber                 \\
  \kappa           & = \sqrt{2} G_F M_Z^2 / (4 \pi \alpha) \;.
\end{align}

Useful constants are given in the tables below.

\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{llll}
    Fermions                       & $Q_f$          & $V_f$                                            & $A_f$          \\ \hline
    $u$, $c$, $t$                  & $+\frac{2}{3}$ & $(+\frac{1}{2} - \frac{4}{3} \sin ^2 \theta_W )$ & $+\frac{1}{2}$ \\
    $d$, $s$, $b$                  & $-\frac{1}{3}$ & $(-\frac{1}{2} - \frac{2}{3} \sin ^2 \theta_W )$ & $-\frac{1}{2}$ \\
    $\nu_e$, $\nu_\mu$, $\nu_\tau$ & $0$            & $\frac{1}{2}$                                    & $+\frac{1}{2}$ \\
    $e$, $\mu$, $\tau$             & $-1$           & $(-\frac{1}{2} + 2 \sin ^2 \theta_W )$           & $-\frac{1}{2}$ \\
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{lll}
    Variable             & Symbol                        & Value                                  \\ \hline
    conversion factor    & GeV$^{-2}\leftrightarrow$\,pb & $3.894\times 10^8$\,pb = 1\,GeV$^{-2}$ \\
    $Z$ boson mass       & $M_Z$                         & 91.188\,GeV                            \\
    $Z$ boson width      & $\Gamma_Z$                    & 2.4414\,GeV                            \\
    QED running coupling & $\alpha$                      & $\frac{1}{132.507}$                    \\
    Fermi constant       & $G_F$                         & $1.16639\times 10^{-5}$~GeV$^{-2}$.    \\
    Weinberg angle       & $\sin^2 \theta_W$             & 0.222246                               \\
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{problem}
  Use standard acceptance-rejection Monte Carlo to generate events $(E_\text{CM}, \cos\theta)$ for the Drell--Yan process in a range $E_\text{CM} \in [10, 200]\,\text{GeV}$ and $\cos\theta \in [-1, 1]$.
  Note, usually $E_\text{CM}$ is fixed in an electron-positron collider, but we will consider a range of energies, which is similar to the situation at a hadron collider where the partonic center-of-mass energy is not known exactly.
  Take the event density to be $f(E_\text{CM}, \cos\theta) = 2\pi\,\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}(E_\text{CM}^2, \cos\theta)$, with no extra weight in $E_\text{CM}$, as if every energy in the range were delivered with the same luminosity.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}
  Use the VEGAS Monte Carlo method~\cite{Lepage:1977sw,Lepage:2020tgj} to generate events $(E_\text{CM}, \cos\theta)$ for the Drell--Yan process in a range $E_\text{CM} \in [10, 200]\,\text{GeV}$ and $\cos\theta \in [-1, 1]$.
  Compare how many function evaluations are needed to arrive at the same number of samples, e.g. 10,000.
  Discuss the settings you use for the VEGAS algorithm, i.e. how many iterations, what kind of damping factor, and how many bins, etc.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}
  \emph{Validation.}
  Integrate Eq.~\eqref{eq:partonicxs} over the solid angle to show that
  \begin{equation}
    \sigma(\hat s) = \frac{4\pi\alpha^2}{3\hat s}\,A_0(\hat s), \qquad
    A_\mathrm{FB}(\hat s) \equiv \frac{\sigma_\mathrm{F} - \sigma_\mathrm{B}}{\sigma_\mathrm{F} + \sigma_\mathrm{B}} = \frac{3A_1(\hat s)}{8A_0(\hat s)},
  \end{equation}
  where $\sigma_\mathrm{F}$ ($\sigma_\mathrm{B}$) is the cross section with $\cos\theta > 0$ ($\cos\theta < 0$).
  For the events from both methods, compare the $E_\text{CM}$ distribution with $\sigma(\hat s)$, and the measured $A_\mathrm{FB}$ in bins of $E_\text{CM}$, with statistical uncertainties, with the prediction.
  Where does $A_\mathrm{FB}$ change sign, and why?
  Both methods also estimate the integral of $f$ over the full range: compare these estimates, with uncertainties, with a numerical quadrature of $\int \sigma(E_\text{CM}^2)\, dE_\text{CM}$.
\end{problem}

\noindent\textbf{Possible extensions:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Generate the same process with a general-purpose event generator such as MadGraph5\_aMC@NLO and compare distributions at a fixed $E_\text{CM}$.
  \item Make the hadron-collider analogy real: generate $q\bar q \to Z/\gamma \to \mu^+\mu^-$ in proton-proton collisions by sampling the parton momentum fractions with parton distribution functions, as in Ref.~\cite{Papaefstathiou:2014mya}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Polymer Collapse with MCMC: A Toy Model of Protein Folding}

A protein is a chain of amino acids that, in water, collapses from an extended coil into a compact structure.
Coarse-grained models replace each amino acid with a single bead, and even a \emph{homopolymer}, in which all beads are identical, captures the first step of folding: as the temperature is lowered, the chain undergoes a transition from a swollen coil to a compact globule~\cite{deGennes:1979}.
(Folding into one specific structure requires different interactions between different beads; see the extensions below.)
This project, introduced in Lecture 21, is adapted from a homework assignment on MCMC for protein folding in CS~2840 (Spring 2025).

Consider a chain of $N$ beads at positions $\vec r_1, \dots, \vec r_N$ in three dimensions.
In reduced units ($\epsilon = \sigma = k_B = 1$), the potential energy is
\begin{equation}
  U(\vec x) = \sum_{i=1}^{N-1} k\left(|\vec r_{i+1}-\vec r_i| - r_0\right)^2 + \sum_{|i-j|\geq 2} 4\left[\left(\frac{1}{r_{ij}}\right)^{12} - \left(\frac{1}{r_{ij}}\right)^{6}\right],
\end{equation}
with $k = 50$, $r_0 = 1$, and $r_{ij} = |\vec r_i - \vec r_j|$.
The first term connects consecutive beads with harmonic bonds, which are fixed: they are never formed or broken.
The second is a Lennard-Jones interaction between all other pairs of beads.
The equilibrium distribution is $\pi(\vec x) \propto e^{-U(\vec x)/T}$, and the size of the chain is measured by its radius of gyration,
\begin{equation}
  R_g^2 = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\vec r_i - \vec r_\text{cm}|^2, \qquad \vec r_\text{cm} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \vec r_i.
\end{equation}

Starter code is available at \url{https://github.com/jmduarte/phys142/blob/main/assignments/final_projects/polymer_mcmc.py}.
It implements $U$, $R_g^2$, and a single-bead Metropolis sampler~\cite{Metropolis:1953} with a symmetric Gaussian proposal.
It is correct but slow.
For Problems B--D you will need to speed it up, e.g., by computing the energy change of a single-bead move in $O(N)$ rather than $O(N^2)$ and compiling the inner loop with Numba.
Report the speedup you achieve.

\begin{problem}
  \emph{Validation.}
  For $N=2$, the chain is a single bond.
  Show that the bond length $d$ is distributed as $P(d) \propto d^2 e^{-k(d-r_0)^2/T}$, and explain the origin of the factor $d^2$.
  Show that $\langle d\rangle \approx r_0 + T/(k r_0)$ to leading order in $T/(k r_0^2)$.
  At $T=1$, compute $\langle d \rangle$ by numerical quadrature and compare it with your MCMC estimate.
  Your MCMC error bar must account for autocorrelation between samples, e.g., using blocking~\cite{Flyvbjerg:1989} or the integrated autocorrelation time.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}
  \emph{Coil--globule transition.}
  For $N=16$ and $N=32$, scan the temperature over $0.4 \leq T \leq 5$.
  Plot $\langle R_g^2\rangle^{1/2}$, the energy per bead $\langle U\rangle/N$, and the heat capacity per bead $C_V/N = (\langle U^2\rangle - \langle U\rangle^2)/(N T^2)$ versus $T$, all with error bars (use a jackknife or bootstrap for $C_V$).
  Estimate the temperature at which the chain collapses.
  Does $C_V$ have a clear peak at the collapse? Discuss.
  Describe your strategy for the initial configuration, burn-in, step size (and resulting acceptance rate), number of sweeps, and thinning (if any).
  Plot the integrated autocorrelation time of $R_g^2$ versus $T$ and discuss what it implies for how long you must run.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}
  \emph{The Hastings correction.}
  Replace the symmetric proposal with a biased one: displace the chosen bead by $\vec\delta \sim \mathcal N(\mu\hat x, s^2 \mathbb{1})$ with $\mu = s$.
  Since $q(\vec x'|\vec x)\neq q(\vec x|\vec x')$, the acceptance probability must include the Hastings factor~\cite{Hastings:1970},
  \begin{equation}
    A(\vec x\to\vec x') = \min\left(1, e^{-\Delta U/T}\,\frac{q(\vec x|\vec x')}{q(\vec x'|\vec x)}\right).
  \end{equation}
  Derive $q(\vec x|\vec x')/q(\vec x'|\vec x)$ for this proposal.
  For $N=16$ at $T=1$, compare $\langle R_g^2\rangle$ and the diagonal components $\langle S_{xx}\rangle$, $\langle S_{yy}\rangle$, $\langle S_{zz}\rangle$ of the gyration tensor, $S_{\alpha\beta} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i (r_{i,\alpha} - r_{\text{cm},\alpha})(r_{i,\beta} - r_{\text{cm},\beta})$, from three samplers: symmetric, biased without the Hastings factor, and biased with it.
  Which agree with each other? What happens to the acceptance rate and the autocorrelation time?
  Finally, show that for $N=2$ the biased sampler gives the correct $\langle d\rangle$ even without the Hastings factor, and explain why.

  \begin{hint}
    Compare averages, e.g., $\langle S_{xx}\rangle$ with $\langle S_{yy}\rangle$, rather than the average of a ratio such as $\langle S_{xx}/S_{yy}\rangle$.
  \end{hint}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}
  \emph{Scaling.}
  At a high temperature ($T=4$, swollen coil) and a low temperature ($T=0.4$, compact globule), measure $\langle R_g^2\rangle^{1/2}$ for $N = 8$, 16, and 32, and fit $R_g \propto N^\nu$.
  Compare your exponents with $\nu \approx 0.588$ for a self-avoiding walk, $\nu = 1/2$ for an ideal chain (the $\theta$ point), and $\nu = 1/3$ for a compact globule~\cite{deGennes:1979}.
  How does the autocorrelation time grow with $N$?
  Discuss finite-size effects and the limitations of single-bead moves.
  Including $N=64$ is optional and will likely require one of the extensions below.
\end{problem}

\noindent\textbf{Possible extensions:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Pivot moves: rotate the part of the chain on one side of a randomly chosen bead by a random rotation~\cite{Madras:1988}.
        Show that this proposal is symmetric, and compare autocorrelation times with single-bead moves, especially for large $N$ at high $T$.
  \item Parallel tempering at low temperatures, where single-bead moves get stuck.
  \item Heteropolymers: make the Lennard-Jones well depth depend on the bead types (e.g., hydrophobic and polar beads), and study whether a specific compact structure emerges.
  \item Start from the $C_\alpha$ trace of a real protein from the Protein Data Bank, and discuss why a homopolymer model does not keep it folded.
\end{itemize}
